# Strategy

## Is it actually a strategy?

A real strategy has three parts:

1. **Diagnosis:** the critical challenge, named plainly.
2. **Guiding policy:** an approach that rules things out.
3. **Coherent actions:** specific steps with an owner, budget and date.

Warning signs of a bad strategy: fluff that collapses when restated plainly, no named obstacle, goals dressed up as strategy, a 40-item list.

- **Negation test:** if the opposite statement sounds absurd ("we will be low quality"), the original says nothing.
- Always write a **"we will not"** list.
- Argue against your own favourite option before recommending it.

## Positioning (in this order)

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what would the best-fit customer do if this brand didn't exist? Include doing nothing, a spreadsheet, hiring someone.
2. **Unique attributes:** only things that pass the "only we" test. "Better" isn't unique.
3. **Value:** for each attribute ask "so what?" until you reach real value. Aim for 2 to 4 value themes, each with proof.
4. **Best-fit customers:** traits you can spot before talking to them, plus who is not a fit.
5. **Market frame:** compete head-on, own a sub-category, or create a new one (new categories cost time and money to explain). Test: does a prospect get it in 30 seconds?

Trends support a position; they are never the core of it.

Statement: "For [best-fit customer], [brand] is the [category] that [main value], unlike [alternative] which [limitation]."

### Positioning workshop (3 hours, client-ready)

| Time | Session |
|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:15 | Framing |
| 0:15 to 0:45 | Alternatives: silent sticky notes, cluster, check against data, dot vote |
| 0:45 to 1:15 | Attributes: brainstorm, then "could alternative A claim this?" Failures go to table stakes |
| 1:15 to 1:25 | Break |
| 1:25 to 1:50 | Value: so-what chains, rank the themes |
| 1:50 to 2:10 | Best-fit: "[role] at [type of company] with [traits] facing [trigger]" |
| 2:10 to 2:35 | Market frame and trends |
| 2:35 to 2:55 | Draft the statement and one headline |
| 2:55 to 3:00 | Owners and a 90-day review date |

Pre-read sent 1 to 2 weeks ahead: interview summaries, wins and losses, competitor matrix, current positioning. When people disagree, defer to customer evidence. If an attribute is genuinely debated, it isn't unique.

## Competitive and cultural landscape

- Three sets: direct competitors, indirect alternatives, and aspirational brands from other categories.
- Compare experience and feeling, not feature lists: interaction style, emotional register, what's table stakes, what nobody does well.
- Review full journeys, not single screens.
- For culturally-driven brands, also map the community: who they follow, what they wear and buy, where they gather, the language they use, what they'd find try-hard.

## Tie design to business outcomes

- For every piece of work, ask which line it moves: conversion, retention, order value, referral, or cost (support volume, churn).
- Frame as a bet: "We're betting X moves Y. Not doing it costs Z."
- Some decisions are craft decisions. Say so rather than inventing a metric.
- Ask the client what success looks like in 90 days.

## Other tools (use when they fit)

- **Strategy canvas and ERRC:** plot competing factors, then decide what to eliminate, reduce, raise and create.
- **Problem framing:** assumptions, stakeholders, problem statement, constraints, success criteria, principles, then priorities.
- **Principles:** each has a short title, statement, reason, example, counter-example and trade-off. Rank them for when they conflict. If nobody would disagree with a principle, it's too vague.

## Proposals and strategy documents

- Lead with the diagnosis, not with our process.
- After any recommendation, estimate or proposal, add 2 or 3 specific "worth a second look" questions: the assumptions most likely to be wrong.
- Before sending, have a fresh agent with no context read it and say what it thinks the document asks for. Fix any gap.
